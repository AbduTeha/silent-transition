## THREAD 1 — Robot unit economics: the BOM, the learning curve, and the fleet business model

**The bill of materials.** The canonical Y0 general-purpose platform ("C-bot," the industry's term for the human-scale universal unit — the *Kinetiq K3*, *OmniFleet Handles-4*, *Huaqing HG-2* class) cost **$18,500 in BOM** to build in 2034. Decomposition: actuators and transmission 38% (planetary roller screws, harmonic drives, frameless torque motors — the Chinese supplier base held ~60% of world actuator volume by Y5, which is the quiet geopolitical fact under the whole transition); end-effectors and hands 12% (the last hard problem — tactile fingertip arrays at $2,100/pair in 2034, $240 by 2044); batteries and power 9% (semi-solid-state, ~1.1 kWh, 6-hour duty); sensors and compute 14% (solid-state lidar, event cameras, 400-TOPS edge inference); structure and castings 11% (the mega-casting revolution arriving from the car industry); final assembly 8%; test and burn-in 8%. Wright's law held almost perfectly: **21% cost decline per doubling of cumulative production** carried BOM from $18.5k (Y0) through $6.9k (Y8, ~80M cumulative) to $3.1k (Y15) and $1,700 at plateau (Y30) — all 2026 dollars.

**The loaded hour.** Canon prices ($3.80 at Y3 → $0.90 at Y10 → $0.40 at Y20) decompose as:

| Component | Y3 | Y10 | Y20 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capex amortization | $1.85 | $0.32 | $0.14 |
| Maintenance & parts | $0.75 | $0.22 | $0.09 |
| Insurance (robot bonds) | $0.50 | $0.12 | $0.05 |
| Energy | $0.45 | $0.18 | $0.09 |
| Supervision compute | $0.10 | $0.03 | $0.02 |
| Fleet overhead | $0.15 | $0.03 | $0.01 |
| **Total (canon)** | **$3.80** | **$0.90** | **$0.40** |

Note the composition shift: at Y3 the robot is a *capital* good (49% of the hour is amortization); by Y10 it is an *operating* good (maintenance and energy dominate) — which is why the leasing market (below) peaked exactly in the Y4–Y9 window and why secondhand fleets became the Y15 story.

**The business model's three acts.** *Act I, Robot-as-Capex (Y0–Y2):* direct sales at $30–60k list, bought by the firms that bought forklifts — the era of the pilot deployment, the RFP, the integrator. *Act II, Fleet-as-Service (Y2–Y9):* the consortia's land grab. OmniFleet's standard **Fleet Agreement** — five-year exclusivity, per-hour pricing, all telemetry assigned to the fleet — captured 41% of US robot-hours by Y11 (canon) and drew the antitrust complaint: the telemetry assignment clause, internal memos showed, was the moat ("utilization is the product," runs the trial's most-quoted exhibit). *Act III, the Exchange (Y12+):* after the Fleet Protocol (2048) mandated interoperable dispatch layers and telemetry portability, fleet-hours became a **spot market** — the Fleet Exchange (Chicago, 2051) quotes robot-hours like cloud compute, with a demand-response tier in which idle fleets bid down prices nightly. The 2054 "Labor Demand Response" programs — cities buying discounted idle fleet-hours for public works during troughs — are the utility industry's demand-side management, applied to labor.

**Why effective FTE is 2.4, not 3.2** (errata 3, adjudicated in 08 §13): maintenance downtime 8%, task-change latency 11% (end-effector swaps, model reloads, site re-mapping — the friction Baumol would have loved), idle gaps 25% (the demand-limited regime, dominant after Y10), energy and siting constraints 4%. The pass's reframing is the thread's punchline: **through Y10 the industry sold scarcity; after Y10 it sold utilization** — and the entire political economy of the 2050s (the Fleet Levy's volatile base, the Double the Dividend fights, the Idle Fleet's politics) sits on that flip.

**The players at Y10, robot-hours share, world:** CGRG/Huaqing 34% · OmniFleet 19% · Kinetiq 11% · Atlas Global 8% · Meridian Industrial 7% · Ampersand (consumer; not in the industrial exchange) 6% · the value tier (ex-Unitree lineage, tariff-sheltered regional brands) 9% · regional integrators 6%. Fleet-hours, by then, trade as a commodity; the consortia's margins had fallen from 34% (Y9 peak) to 11% (Y15) — the Fleet Protocol's whole purpose, achieved at the exact moment the Idle Fleet made margins irrelevant anyway. The secondhand market deserves its own sentence: by Y15, a decade-old C-bot with 40,000 hours on the clock trades at $900 — the fleet economy's used-car market, and the reason Global South leapfrog (08 §10) was capital-cheap.

## THREAD 2 — The American Dividend Act, anatomized

**The legislative archaeology.** Four bills in sequence. (1) The **Fiscal Emergency Act** (Feb 2044, Boone signing, Marsh voting): temporary fleet windfall levies (34% of consortium excess margins) funding the gap — the stool's training wheels, lapsed 2047. (2) The Marsh platform's **"Own the Future"** framework (campaign 2044): dividend + VAT + land tax, drafted with the Brookings-Urban Institute working group (the "Harmon papers," leaked Aug 2044, supplied the fiscal arithmetic below). (3) The **Marsh–Keene bargain** (autumn 2045): Speaker Ada Ruth Keene (R-PA, Steelman Coalition) delivered 118 populist votes for the VAT in exchange for the family top-ups (the "bassinet bonus," +$1,400/child under 6 — later credited, alongside crèche-robots, with the US fertility uptick), the Copper Card's physical-iconography requirement (the penny-bronze card; 41% of eligible adults kept the physical card through Y30), and the Fleet Levy's 2048 phase-in rather than 2046. (4) **The Act itself**, signed March 14, 2046; first payments January 1, 2047; 258 million adults on the rolls; take-up 99.4% (opt-out 0.6%, mostly the devout and the dissolved).

**The fiscal tables, corrected per 08 §7.**

| Year | VAT (rate) | LVT (effective capture) | Fleet Levy | Energy levy | Residual income/other | **Receipts** | Dividend | Health (UCA) | Defense | All else | **Outlays** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2047 | $3.0T (14%) | $340B (75%) | — | $210B | $2.4T | **$5.95T** | $2.63T | $3.1T | $0.7T | $1.3T | **$7.73T** |
| 2054 | $4.4T (18%) | $460B (58%) | $780B | $260B | $2.2T | **$8.1T** | $3.56T | $3.4T | $0.75T | $1.6T | **$9.31T** |
| 2064 | $5.2T (18%) | $520B (41%) | $810B | $220B | $2.6T | **$9.35T** | $4.16T | $3.9T | $0.8T | $2.1T | **$10.96T** |

The deficits (1.5–1.7% of GDP through the 2050s, closing to 0.4% by 2064) are financed at r* ≈ −0.3% against the robot savings glut (Thread 8) — the arithmetic that made the whole settlement *cheap*, and the fact the fleet lobbies never successfully weaponized: you cannot run a debt-scare on money that costs less than nothing.

**The dividend formula.** The Act's quiet genius is the **Fleet-Indexed Dividend Formula**: the dividend is not a number but a *share* — 0.9% of GDP at enactment, raised to 1.2% by the Double the Dividend Act (2056) — computed quarterly from VAT receipts plus the Fleet Levy plus the LVT's floor share. Every productivity gain in the fleet economy flows to the formula automatically. The politics this created: the dividend never needs re-authorizing (no Social-Security-style crisis), but its *share* is permanently contestable — which is why US elections after 2046 are, underneath everything, share fights, and why the 2056 landslide (Thibodeaux, 412 EVs) happened in the first post-scarcity decade rather than the third: the formula made the stakes legible (06 §6's legibility finding, applied).

**The accidents that held it** (canon per 08 §5): the 2047 **Dividend Repeal Act** — passed the House 231–204 on fleet-lobby money, defeated in the Senate 51–49 by Senator Harmon Voss (R-NE, retiring, fleet-manufacturing state, whose final floor speech — "my constituents' dividends out-pay their old wages, and I will not take the paycheck of the future away to save the pride of the past" — is the Act's engraved defense); and *Okafor v. Idaho* (2052), striking the Idaho Two-Tier loyalty-dividend scheme 8–1 under HE Amendment Section 1's adjudication clause. Three accidents, one settlement: the emergency window (2046), Voss's vote, the courts. The sim's honest annotation: the ADA is a building whose blueprints are less interesting than its fire record.

## THREAD 3 — China's Digital Mandate: CGRG, Provision, and the Second Mandate

**CGRG.** The China General Robotics Group consolidated 214 robot and automation firms by decree in 2037 — Huaqing General Robotics as the operating core, Unitree-lineage value brands as the export tier, the mega-casting and actuator supplier base nationalized into the group's "Tier-0" division. Capitalization ¥13T (Y5) → ¥41T (Y12); production 15M/yr (Y5, half the world's) → 90M/yr (Y10); global installed share 34% (Y10, Thread 1). The state's lever was not ownership of firms but ownership of *demand*: the Domestic Fleet Circulation Rule (2038) required any robot sold into China's state-linked economy to run the **Zhuangzhou attestation stack**, which made CGRG the only insurer-acceptable supplier inside the bloc — attestation as industrial policy, the sim's most-copied Chinese innovation.

**The Provision System's mechanics.** In kind, guaranteed: housing (fleet-built, 22 m²/adult baseline, expandable by points), calories (the "basket card"), transit, basic care, education. In points: 保障分 ("guarantee points," colloquially *bao fen*) earned by residence, contribution-program service, and civic participation; spent on premium consumption, travel, larger housing. Cash dividend: ¥1,100/month adult average by Y12 (the Wuxi Standard's rate), raised by fleet surplus. The demand-management layer — **Leisure Scheduling** (staggered national holidays, by province cluster, to flatten consumption peaks) — is the system's most exported and most disliked feature, and the reason the world's tourism map has five Chinese seasons.

**Wuxi.** The Experiment (2039, 12.6M people, ¥41B/yr) ran eighteen months early and reported, honestly: consumption +23%, Gray Slump indicators materially better than control prefectures, TFR +0.08 (the disappointment every pro-natal program shares), labor-force participation stable at 34% (the Human-in-the-Loop class), and one finding the Politburo underlined: *provision without participation produced passivity within two years* — the Wuxi "sitting villages," districts where point-earning fell 40% as the basket made earning optional. The Wuxi Standard (2041) therefore baked in the contribution layer: bao fen weighted toward service, teaching, care rotations — China's version of HE Amendment Section 3, discovered empirically.

**The strains, honestly listed** (errata 1): the Harbin riots (2043, provision-recalibration protest, 200+ dead, the system's Kent State); the "Shenzhen gap" (2042–44: private fleet owners — the drone-and-AGV millionaires of the 2020s — refusing CGRG conversion; resolved by golden-share conversions at 30% state equity, which is why Shenzhen's robot billionaires are, at Y30, a peculiar class: rich, listed, and non-voting); the hukou equalization (2043) as the largest internal redistribution in Chinese history (¥2.1T/yr); the Hangzhou succession (2047), in which the technocratic faction under Premier-designate Lin Wenzhou beat the security faction by exactly the Wei Jun logic — the fleet stock is the mandate, and you do not burn the mandate.

**The Second Mandate.** Per 08 §4's canon: the system's own theorists (the "provision-legitimacy" school at the Central Party School) concluded by the early 2050s that delivery alone decays into plateau, and plateau into claim-collapse. The answer, piloted 2057 in six provinces: the **Wenceng chambers** (文衡, "weighing the civil") — sortition-flavored consultative assemblies with real power over *local* provision allocation (which hospitals get the care-fleet hours; which lines the Leisure Scheduling lights first), deliberately modeled, the Party School's papers admit, on the Sénat Citoyen and the Yaw Chambers. At Y30 the chambers cover 40% of prefectures, their approval ratings (60s) out-poll every party organ, and the interesting question the sim leaves open — 02's deadline, still ticking — is whether the Party that built the world's fastest fleet can build, in time, the world's slowest thing: a valve.

## THREAD 4 — India's 700-million question

**The exposure.** 700M workers; 85% informal; services exports $250B/yr at Y0 (18% of GDP, the world's largest such share) falling to $155B by Y3 — the Service Export Cliff's ground zero; remittances $115B → $70B by Y5 as the Gulf and the diaspora themselves were displaced. The IT services industry — 4.8M employed, TCS/Infosys/Wipro the flagships — went from growth engine to **the bench generations**: 1.9M remaining by Y3, average utilization 41%, the "non-renewal trapdoor" (08 §3) hitting India's exact demographic sweet spot — the 2005–2015 engineering-cohort, 11 million strong, mid-career, mortgaged, the Squandered Ascent with Indian characteristics.

**The politics.** The farmer protests of 2036 (the MSP demonstrations metastasizing into the first "wage-of-the-future" movement); the **National Robotics Mission** (2036: PLI-Robotics subsidies, 40% capex support, aimed — with complete success — at making India a robot *builder* rather than a robot *buyer*); the **Right to Contribution Act** (2040): 90M enrolled in river cleanup, afforestation, care queues, census work, at ₹8,000/month + ration points — working badly through 2043 (absenteeism 44%, the "attendance economy" of ghost musters), then moderately after the 2044 redesign (local panchayat administration, work menus, the "contribution wage" re-indexed). The **Contribution Riots** (2044, Patna, Nagpur) forced the redesign — India's Grid Riots — and the Election Commission's stubborn independence through both the 2039 and 2044 cycles is the sim's quiet evidence for why India appears in the *adapted* column at all.

**The federal spectrum.** Kerala ran the co-op dividend: panchayat fleet trusts, the full dividend floor by 2048, the subcontinent's best WAS recovery. Gujarat ran the fleet-manufacturing state: the **Coimbatore Robot Cluster** (2.1M robots/yr by Y15; the actuator Tier-0 that broke Chinese supplier dominance inside the Non-Aligned world), wages in the plant towns rising *through* the transition. The spectrum's lesson, now taught everywhere: there was no Indian transition; there were thirty, and the federation — criticized for a century as friction — was the shock absorber.

**The recovery.** "Crafted in India" (human-premium services exports: $180B by Y20 — attested software guilds, certified care exports, the Handmark trade belt); robot manufacturing (the Mission's success: India is the NACM's arsenal); GDP growth averaging 5.8%/yr Y10–Y20; population peaking 1.53B in 2047; the Walkers' generation entering politics by the 2050s (the "Contribution Cohorts" dominate state elections from 2051). The honest annotation from 08 §10: India is the sim's largest *lagged leapfrog* — a decade of transition pain (the Long Walk's eastern anchor, ~1.9M South Asian excess deaths' largest share) followed by the fastest industrialization-without-labor ever recorded, which is either the transition's best news or its most unsettling precedent, depending on which of the still-lagging corridor states can copy it.

## THREAD 5 — *United States v. OmniFleet* and the Fleet Protocol saga

**The complaint** (filed March 2045, DOJ Antitrust Division + FTC, the first case ever pleaded as "monopolization of general labor services"): OmniFleet's 41% of US robot-hours (canon); the **Fleet Agreement's exclusivity ties** (a customer taking fleet management takes OmniFleet dispatch, telemetry, maintenance, and insurance as a bundle); **predatory per-hour pricing** against independent fleet operators in 2041–43 (the "dumping summers": OmniFleet undercut at-cost 19 months, then absorbed 31 rivals); and the **telemetry moat** — task-performance data from 4.1M units feeding the dispatch models that competitors could not match, the flywheel the internal strategy memo named in the trial's defining exhibit: *"Utilization is the product. The robot is the packaging."*

**The trial.** Judge Naomi Reyes, SDNY, 2046–48. The government's economists (the "Harmon school" from the ADA working group) framed the market as *general labor services*, a definition OmniFleet's counsel called "the antitrust equivalent of suing electricity." The turning exhibits: the dumping-summers pricing ledgers, and the **Ghost Fleet memo** — not the criminal ghost fleets of 06, but OmniFleet's internal name for units withheld from dispatch to keep spot prices elevated during the 2043 overcapacity (the Idle Fleet's first criminal appearance: *withholding* as the moat's last redoubt). Reyes's summary judgment on liability (Feb 2048) rejected breakup — "the court will not divide the machines; it will divide the *control*" — and the consent decree (May 2048) became the **Fleet Protocol**:

1. **Dispatch interoperability**: any certified dispatch layer may command any registered fleet; switching costs must fall below defined thresholds (the "two-click fleet change" rule).
2. **Telemetry portability**: task-performance data flows to a pooled, audited trust (operated by the new **Fleet Protocol Foundation**) on 90-day lag — competitors train on the moat.
3. **Non-discrimination**: per-hour pricing published; no loyalty tiering; no exclusive bundles.
4. **CAOB audit rights**: the Community Algorithmic Oversight Boards (06 §2) get standing fleet-log access — the antitrust settlement quietly becoming police-reform law.
5. **Foundation stewardship of Pantheon OS**: open core, attestation-mandated, the Foundation's board tripartite (consortia / states / sortition-selected public seats).

**Effects, quantified.** Fleet-switching costs fell ~80% by Y15; the Fleet Exchange (2051) spot market exists because the Protocol made hourly pricing *legible*; consortia margins 34% (Y9) → 11% (Y15); OmniFleet's market cap fell 46% in the decree week and recovered above it by Y15 — the classic remedy paradox, the firm more profitable as a protocol-compliant utility than as a fief. The doctrine exported within eighteen months: EU fleet directive (2049), the AFC's adoption-by-reference (2046–48), the Hong Kong Fleet Convention's levy harmonization (2048) written on the Protocol's registry spine.

**The second-order history.** The Protocol is the case where the Varoufakis-Brandt critique (04 §6) was *tested and vindicated*: the fiefs could be dissolved by law. It is also the case that made the Idle Fleet governable — an interoperable fleet is a schedulable fleet, and the 2054 Labor Demand Response programs assume the Protocol the way the grid assumes the plug. And it created the era's most-quoted legal sentence, Reyes's, which the *Cambridge History* uses as the volume-2 epigraph: *"A fleet is a commons wearing a corporate seal. The seal may stay. The commons belongs to everyone whose labor it replaced."*
