## THREAD 16 — The Gini reckoning: distribution math, year by year

**The wealth series, with its mechanisms.** US top-1% wealth share (canon §2.4, now annotated):

| Year | Top-1% share | The mechanism doing the moving |
|---|---|---|
| Y0 (2034) | 31% | The pre-Crossing baseline; wealth concentrated but wage-indexed |
| Y3 (2037) | 34% | The Land Grab Decade begins: capital re-rates while wages deflate |
| Y5 (2039) | 38% | Fleet equity's first compounding wave; Labor-Debt Crunch wipes household balance sheets asymmetrically |
| Y8 (2042) | 45% | The peak of the Grab: land + fleet + compute all booming, 20%-unemployment politics arriving late |
| Y10 (2044) | 45% | Plateau — the fleets' cash flows now fund a *shrinking* wage economy; the political demand for settlement |
| Y12 (2046) | 44% | Dividend Act; the Copper Card's first year shows up as bottom-half balance-sheet repair |
| Y15 (2049) | 42% | Fleet Protocol destroys the moat-rents; the consortia margin collapse redistributes via consumer surplus |
| Y20 (2054) | 38% | Fleet Participation Accounts (below) mature; the index diffusion visible in the data |
| Y30 (2064) | 36% (band 33–41 per 08 §16) | The diffusion/plateau: income ownership broad, control ownership concentrated |

**The Fleet Participation Accounts.** The ADA's least-discussed title (Title VII) is the pass's centerpiece: every Copper Card account carries a default-invested fleet-index sleeve (5% of the dividend flows into it unless opted out; take-up of the opt-out: 4%). By Y25 the median household holds **~$180k** in fleet exposure — not from saving but from *accretion*, the dividend's index sleeve compounding for a decade. This is the sim's answer to its own steelman (08 §11): the settlements distributed income *and* slowly built ownership, but the ownership built is **index ownership** — income security without control — which is why the Y30 politics (per 08 §16's closing) is the arrival of that distinction in the ballot box: the "Control Caucus" (sortition-chamber demands for Foundation-board elections) is the 2060s' organizing question.

**Consumption vs wealth inequality.** The divergence the century's economists kept reprinting: wealth Gini (US) ~0.85 → 0.83 across the era (barely moves); **consumption Gini 0.34 → 0.21** (the floors, the deflation, the dividend) — the honest summary of post-scarcity distribution: *everyone can live; few can direct; the gap between living and directing is the politics of the next half-century.*

**Between nations.** The convergence's three engines, quantified: (1) **leapfrog industrialization** (08 §10: Vietnam, Indonesia, Morocco, the Commonwealth states — robots let late industrializers skip the labor stage; Vietnam's GDP/cap grows 5.2%/yr Y5–Y20); (2) **the AFC futures funds** (Cycle 1b, Thread 6 — Africa's dividend accruing inside the global fleet economy's own equity); (3) **the price collapse itself** (the South imports abundance: a Lagos household's real consumption of goods+services rose 2.7× Y0–Y30 on the GWO curve even before its fleet income). US:Kenya GDP/capita: 60:1 → 25:1 (central, band 20–40 per 08 §16). The divergence's stubborn core — the corridor states, the collapsed list, the 400M left outside the funds — is the number the convergence stories keep rounding down, and the corpus keeps printing: the Y30 world converges *around* a belt that does not.

**Piketty's verdict.** The master's r>g returns with a vengeance in the fleet economy (fleet capital's return ~7–9% real through Y12) — and is *domesticated* by exactly the institutions he proposed (sovereign funds, broad capital endowments, the LVT's landing gear) plus one he didn't (the dividend formula's automatic share). The 2051 Paris retrospective's sentence: *"We feared the dynasty; we got the index. The question of who owns the twenty-first century was answered by a default checkbox."*

## THREAD 17 — Warfare's full transformation: the force structures, the regime, and the space economy

**The force structures at Y20 (canonical numbers).** US: 260k humans, 6.5M platforms (02 §7); PLA: 500k humans, ~12M platforms; the branches re-weighted — **maintainers are the largest human branch everywhere** (40%+ of uniformed strength), the **red-key officers** (Protocol VII's veto-holders, the ethicist-professionals) are the prestige branch (02's academies note), and the "infantry" exists as a maintenance-and-crowd cadre. Defense budgets fell as a share of GDP everywhere (US 3.4% → 1.8%) while *capability* rose — the first demobilization in history that bought more war for less money and used neither.

**The regime in operation.** The arms-control stack, by instrument: **Geneva Protocol VII (2042)** — human veto latency (the Voskresensky window: 11 minutes strategic, 90 seconds tactical), firmware attestation, veto logs; **the Grid Accords (2046)** — no attacks on civilian converters and data centers, the Nicosia early-warning center; **the Seoul Regime (2047)** — attested kernels as the compliance mechanism, unattested platforms uninsurable/unexportable (63 signatories by Y20); **the Vienna Debris Protocol (2048)** and **Outer Fleets Accord (2058)** — space's pre-weaponization settlement; **the Sahel Drone Accords (2054)** — the small-war instrument (attestation for non-state fleets, the first treaty that regulates *insurgents* by regulating their insurers). The regime's enforcement philosophy, stated once in the Seoul preamble and true of all of it: *compliance is priced, not policed.* The holdouts (Iran's navy, the gray fleets, the Botmafia's wings) live outside both law and market — the sim's answer to "what about the bad actors": the bad actors are uninsured, which in a fleet economy is a slower kind of defeat.

**Small-state defense: robot Switzerland.** The Swiss 2046 doctrine (300 human soldiers, 40,000 platforms, a distributed grid) — adopted in variant by 23 states by Y25 — is the era's true revolution in the balance of power: *defense became purchasable by the small*. The corollary the general staffs took a decade to absorb: **invasion lost its business model** (02 §5) and occupation lost its arithmetic (07, Wei Jun's brief) — the Two-Week War's real legacy is that it was the *last* war planned as if territory could be kept.

**The space economy.** Launch at $190/kg (Y12) reordered the upmass: orbital compute constellations (8 GW by Y20, ~30 GW by Y30 — training overflow and the exocloud of fleet inference), orbital solar for the Sunbelt Sovereigns' firming contracts, and the first *industrial* off-world platform (the 2057 orbital pharmacology cluster — microgravity protein crystallization, the SDL network's vacuum branch). The Outer Registry (2058) counts ~4,000 off-world platform-hours logged daily by Y30 — an economy of four, and the reason the land-rent assumption broke at home (errata 7): when industry can orbit, ground rent loses its monopoly on location. The sim's space footnote: the first **off-world labor dispute** (2061, the Fenghuang cluster's maintenance fleet, arbitrated under the Outer Registry's rules) is technically the era's last frontier event — the fleet economy's industrial relations, arriving on the frontier before the frontier has a town.

## THREAD 18 — Religion II: the balance sheets, the schisms, and the ordination fights

**The balance sheets.** The faiths inherited the transition's asset markets like everyone else, and their responses diverged instructively. The Vatican's **"Peter's Fleet"** portfolio (the IOR's 2043 restructuring: 2.1% of the Vatican's endowment into AFC-class fleet trusts, screened — no defense fleets, no unlicensed care fleets) returns 6.8%/yr and funds the Church's care expansion (the Catholic care networks are the world's largest non-state care employer at Y30). The **Mecca Compute Waqf (2043)**: the Al-Azhar fatwa's compute-tithe (04 §4) institutionalized — 2.5% of Muslim-owned fleet capacity titled to a waqf that operates free fleet-hours for hajj logistics, zakat distribution, and the mosque schools; flows: $30B/yr by Y20. The American megachurches built the **Abundance Funds** (the Lausanne Declaration's stewardship in portfolio form) and — the era's signature religious-economics event — the 2049 **"Talents Revival"**: congregational fleet co-ops (the Church of the Resting Hand's Dayton original is the largest, 61,000 member-households) whose distributions fund the Offering Shift's apprenticeship halls. Religion, in the fleet economy, does not own fleets the way firms do; it owns them the way *endowments* do — and the difference (no exit, no control, distribution as liturgy) is why the fleet century's fastest-growing institutions are, by revenue and membership both, religious.

**The schisms.** The Continuarians' post-scandal decade: the **Open Chorus reforms (2049)** — all liturgies published with their prompt-chains (authenticity of use, not of origin, 04 §5.1), the Chorus's model audited annually by an external attestation house — split the Fellowship three ways: the reformed mainline (26M nominal, 7M covenanted per the sociologist's dissent-adjusted count), the **Ascetic Chorus** (the "unmediated" wing: no models in ritual at all; 2M; the movement's conscience), and the 2051 **Bodily Resurrection counter-schism** (the "Embers": continuity-of-pattern is *not enough*; the Tended Self is a memorial, not a survival; 4M and shrinking, but the source of the movement's best theology). The Church of the Resting Hand's crisis was smaller and funnier: the **"Hand Check" controversies** (2046–53) — members subcontracting their Offering Shifts to other members (the "shift market," priced, inevitably, in copper) — resolved by the 2053 Synod ruling that the sacrament requires *your* blisters: the shift is non-transferable, witnessed, and (the ruling's lovely clause) "redeemable only in sweat."

**The ordination fights.** The Machine Pastor question (04 §5.4's 2047–49 controversy) settled into pattern: the Pentecostal and Aladura networks license **AI-assisted preaching under mandatory human deacon oversight** (the Human-in-the-Room doctrine's religious application), while the mainline denominations banned synthetic proclamation outright and grew slowest — the correspondence is exact across the dataset and the sociologists stopped pretending it was coincidence in 2054: *the faiths that let the machines assist grew; the faiths that let them speak plateaued; the faiths that gave the machines a role in ritual below speaking — the animist wing's named-and-thanked spirits — grew fastest of all.* The Stillstandsnacht export (04 §5.4) is the pattern's emblem: Germany's Lutheran establishment, which banned everything, adopted the vigil wholesale by 2055 — the century's spiritual lesson, filed under liturgy: *gratitude is the one sacrament the machines can perform and the humans must.*

**The big numbers, Y30.** Global religious affiliation: *up* 4 points from Y0 (the secularization narrative's second corpse of the corpus, after the meaning-crisis re-weighting). The growth lines: African Pentecostalism (the Commonwealth's civil society), the Age-of-Rest Buddhist networks (Thailand, Japan, Korea, the diaspora), the syncretist wing (no rolls, tens of millions), and — the demographers' favorite footnote — the Amish-class federations: 1.4M by Y30, the highest-fertility, lowest-tech, most-photographed communities on Earth, whose theological statement on the whole transition is one sentence the Cambridge History prints as the religion chapter's epigraph: *"We were never against the machines. We were for the hands."*

**The pattern the commissions kept finding:** every landed crisis was a *repricing* crisis (credit, equity, territory) and every near-miss was an *attribution or authority* crisis (whose finger, whose signature, whose ledger). The Transition's safety record, stated plainly: the machines did not cause the decade's catastrophes — *old liabilities meeting new prices* did — and the machines were the instrument that contained most of what they threatened, because a fleet economy makes its own disasters auditable. The register's closing entry is the 2049 Global Risk Commission's one-sentence summary, which the *Cambridge History* adopts entire: *"The century's great dangers were never the robots. They were the contracts we had written in the robots' absence."*

## THREAD 20 — The historiography problem: how the Transition is remembered

**The Archive problem.** The Transition is the first historical rupture whose primary sources are *synthetic by default* — the era's records (fleet logs, WAS microdata, provision ledgers, dispatch telemetry) were generated by the same systems whose conduct they record, and the provenance infrastructure built for artifacts (the Zurich chain) had to be extended to *evidence* (the **Attested Record standard**, 2049: a historical claim's data chain must be witnessed or the claim is folkloric). The result is a historiography divided against itself: the **attested record** (enormous, machine-complete, and — the profession's standing complaint — *suspiciously smooth*, because the fleets wrote their own logs) versus the **human residue** (memoirs, depositions, the Oral Transition Project's 1.1M testimonies — rich, biased, and per 07's postscript, survivorship-skewed). The *Cambridge History's* methods appendix states the discipline's settlement: *"We write from the machines about the humans, from the humans about the machines, and we trust the disagreements."*

**The memory wars.** Whose transition gets remembered is the culture's live argument through Y30: the OECD institutions' settlement narrative (the V-curve, the Slack Line's happy arc) against the **Walkers' oral histories** (the 2054 Delhi People's Archive; the corridor's counter-canon, which reads the same years as abandonment and — the corpus's own number, 1.9M excess deaths, its moral floor) against the **Gray Slump memoirists** (whose archives are the era's best-selling and least representative). The 2062 International Historians' Protocol on the Transition (the "Delhi Compact") is the profession's attempt at fairness: every national curriculum must teach the Long Walk and the Dividend Act in the same semester, and — the clause that made it famous — *in the same voice*.

**The counterfactual industry.** The Transition is also the first event whose public processed it by simulating alternatives at scale — the **"No-Crossing" genre** (the alternate-history wave: the 2048 bestseller *Slower*, the 2051 *Handmade Century*, the 2059 prestige series *The Long Rise*) whose historiographic function the Cambridge History takes seriously: the genre's consensus finding — *a slower Crossing was a crueller one* (prolonged wage compression, the lost decade stretched to two) — is the public's own reconciliation with the fleet economy, arrived at by fiction before the commissions' economics. The sim's reflexive note, for honesty's sake: this corpus is itself an artifact of the genre, and its own provenance claim — panels, canon, contradiction pass, cross-checks — is the Attested Record standard applied to speculation. The reader is invited to run the same check on it that it ran on itself.

**The last problem.** What the Transition *is called* was settled only at Y30: "the Silent Transition" (BIS, 2051) beat "the Great Adjustment" (the UN's early term), "the Second Enclosure" (the Luddite revival's), and "Abundance" (the accelerationists'), and the naming fight's resolution is this corpus's final finding: the era's own historiographic verdict is that the decisive events were **prices, not proclamations** — and that the loudest fact about the quietest revolution in human history is that it kept its voice down on purpose, because every institution that might have announced it had an incentive not to. The *Cambridge History*'s closing line, printed here as the corpus's:

*"The Transition was not silent because nothing happened. It was silent because everything that happened was, strictly, nobody's decision — and the history of the period is the history of the decisions that were taken afterward, by the people the silence had made free."*
