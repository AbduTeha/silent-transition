# PHASE 7 — DAILY-LIFE VIGNETTES: FIVE LIVES ACROSS THE TRANSITION
### A panel of the ordinary: Iowa, Bengaluru, Beijing, Lagos, Zwickau — at Year 5, Year 15, and Year 30

---

## Editor's preface (from the *Cambridge History of the Transition*, vol. 3, 2089)

The macro record of the Silent Transition — the price curves, the fleet counts, the treaty texts — survives in abundance, and it is precisely the abundance that makes it useless for the historian's first question, which is not *what happened* but *what it was like to be a person while it happened*. For that question we depend on witnesses, and the witnesses divide into the surveyed and the self-selected: the World Affect Survey's millions, who answered instruments; and the memoirists, who answered their own grief.

The five lives gathered here were chosen by the History's editorial board as a **panel of the ordinary** — not the famous transitionaries (Marsh, Kesler, Mensah, Melnik) but the statistical middle of five very different bells. A farmer whose asset appreciated; a coder whose skill depreciated; a general whose army changed faster than his doctrine; a teenager who was never needed; a mechanic who ended the era tending machines out of love. Between them they span the transition's full ledger: the winner and the loser of the Crossing, the adapter and the captive, the cohort that lost a ladder and the cohort that never believed in ladders at all. Their testimonies — depositions to the 2071–77 Oral Transition Project, cross-attested under the Zurich Protocol's human-recall standard — are reproduced with minimal editing. Where their memories conflict with the record, we have printed both and preferred neither. That is not modesty. It is method: the Transition was a change in what it meant to be needed, and no instrument has ever measured that from the outside.

## 1. Martin "Marty" Ackermann — Sac County, Iowa

### May 2039 — "The land got rich and I got lonesome"

Marty is fifty-eight, and the peak unemployment number on the radio this morning — twenty and a half percent, a figure the anchors say like a weather warning — is somebody else's statistic. In Sac County it is nearly a hundred: there hasn't been a hired hand within forty miles since 2037, and the Custom Ag crews that used to roll up from Texas are metals now, forty of them, walking the Rows like slow bright scarecrows.

What happened to Marty is that he got *rich*, and he can't make it feel like winning. The appraiser from the Farmers Co-op came out in March and said it flat: nine thousand an acre to twenty-eight in five years, because land is the thing the machines can't make and everybody wants to stand on. The tax man takes some of it now — the increment recovery act, Trevor explains, "they only take the part the world gave you" — and the check that comes back on the Copper Card is more than the farm ever cleared in a hail year.

Trevor came home in February. Marketing automation in Chicago, gone in the Junior Freeze like the whole bottom floor of his building; he's twenty-nine and he does the Commons' bookkeeping now, and Marty can't decide if he's proud or heartbroken, watching his son be competent at things no one pays for. In April the two of them drove to Wall Lake and put their names on the articles of the Heartland Commons — forty-one households pooling the fleet leases, machines owned the way their grandfathers owned the tile plow: together, or not at all. Roy Batista's pamphlet is in the truck, dog-eared, the schoolteacher's voice in it plain as rain: *own the bots, or rent your life from whoever does.*

The June before, at the co-op meeting, there'd been talk of the riots — Chicago's depots burning on the news, the open palms on the overpasses. Marty voted with the room for the resolution supporting "economic settlement," then drove home past the agri-fleet charging barn and thought, not for the first time, that he was one of the luckiest ruined men in America.

### October 2049 — "The Combine Guild"

The cinema in Sac City reopened in 2047 — first show since he was a boy — and tonight it's the Fair Leagues regionals, the chase-format combine exhibition, and Marty has an aisle seat because he is, at sixty-eight, something like a local dignitary: master of the Combine Guild, which is written on the door of the old dealership in Handmark-certified paint.

The Guild is his second ascent, and he was as surprised as anyone. It started as eight old men keeping the legacy tractors running — not because the Commons needed them (the fleet diagnoses itself, orders its own parts, and schedules its own service) but because an engine you can hear is a truth you can check. Then the Handmark Trust's assessor came out from Des Moines, ran the attestation rig over Marty's 4020 rebuild — keystrokes, tool logs, the whole session chain — and shook his hand: *certified human restorations, 94 percent manual.* The waiting list now runs to 2041. German collectors. A museum in Osaka. Trevor teases him: "You got certified as the thing they replaced you with, Dad."

The dividend check is $11,900 this year and the Commons' distribution is separate, and the truth is Marty doesn't think about money in months. He thinks in seasons and guild nights and the Tuesday rotation at the food bank, where he owes somebody his Tuesday — a phrase he picked up off one of the relief documentaries and never knew it had already been in a novel and a psychology paper and a president's speech.

His granddaughter — Trevor's girl — is fourteen and wants to run fleets, has the aptitude scores for the dispatch academy in Ames. Marty, who watched robots take the hiring hall and the cattle auction and the grain elevator office, has learned to want what's coming. "The land kept its people by finally needing them less," he says at the guild table, and the young ones write it down like scripture, and he wishes they'd write down the part where it cost.

### March 2064 — "Judging"

Marty is eighty-three, in a scooter at the fence line of the Fair League tractor-pull because the Guild made him judge emeritus and judges emeritus get the shade. The Commons owns the county's fleet now — all 11,000 units, the deed in a trust with the county's name on it — and his granddaughter runs it, and when she rides the field in her dispatch whites the machines part around her kart like fish around a heron.

He is dying of the ordinary kind of time, and he knows it, and he is not afraid, and the only argument he has left with God is inventory: the shops full of tools he taught an apprentice to respect; the attestation seal on every bench; the 4020 in the museum with his name on the chain-of-custody plate. The margaret comes out twice a day with the grain report and his medications and the messages, and he has taught it the grandchildren's names the way the memoirs all say you do — slowly, like planting.

The last thing he dictates to the oral-history robot, because a human transcriber costs extra and this is the one extravagance he's earned, is the sentence they'll put on the school wall:

*"We were the last generation that worked because somebody needed us to. Don't pity us for it. But don't you dare tell me it wasn't the best part."*

## 2. Priya Raghunathan — Bengaluru

### June 2039 — "The wage curve"

Priya is twenty-seven and has been unemployed for eleven months, which in Bengaluru in 2039 is called *freelancing*, and which her mother calls by the old Kannada word for waiting.

The numbers of her descent are exact, because she keeps them in a spreadsheet the way she was trained to keep everything: hired June 2033, fresh out of RV College, at a multinational's development center — ₹9.4 lakh a year, the first salary in her family's history that had a comma in it. Laid off December 2036 in the freeze that never thawed — not a layoff, a *non-renewal*, the entire junior cohort of the floor not re-signed, because in the cognitive sectors there was no cheap-human phase (the economists wrote this later, in the papers she will one day audit; the people lived it as a trapdoor). Then the slide down the wage curve, which the gig platforms priced in real time: ₹800 an hour, then ₹300, then the audit work at four dollars, US clients who pay in the currency of her grandparents' aspirational alphabet.

What she learned in 2039: that the Service Export Cliff the TV anchors argued about was not a national statistic, it was her own payslip drawn large — the back offices of the rich world had been India's factory, and the factory had closed with no smoke. That her parents' flat, mortgaged twice, was worth less than the gold her mother had pawned quietly in 2037, and that she knew about the gold and said nothing. That the night buses home from Electronic City were full of people like her, and that nobody on them said *engineer* anymore.

And that she was, by the standard of the age, one of the lucky ones — because in March a human-premium audit house (EU clients, liability-scared, Insured) had taken her on at a Human-Premium Registry wage to do what the models cannot credibly do: *attest*. To read code with her name attached to the reading. The premium is sixty percent over the robot-quote, and it exists because somewhere in Frankfurt an insurance pool decided that a human signature is worth insuring and a model's is not.

She takes the bus home past the construction site where the metals are raising a tower in eleven days flat, and she lets herself think the thought that the WAS surveys will spend a decade documenting: *I am relieved, and I am less, and I do not know what the second thing is.*

### February 2049 — "The Guild and the book"

The Guild of Attested Systems has forty-one members, a floor above a jeweler on Residency Road, and Priya is its chair, which is a sentence twenty-year-old her would have needed explained: not a company, a *guild* — craft rules, a common fee schedule, a Hall where the apprentices sit the certification, all of it under the "Crafted in India" export mark that the Commerce Ministry's people finally admitted was working. Provenance-audited software: the world's richest buyers pay for the certificate that a person meant it, and Bengaluru — which lost the commodity it had been selling to the whole century — found it had been sitting on the replacement the whole time. *Attention is the only scarcity,* the cultural minister said at the trade fair. *We have a billion.*

She is thirty-seven. She married Arjun at twenty-nine — a Kinetic League coach, the human-vs-robot handicap cricket format, a man whose job is to argue with a machine about what a fair bowler is — and their daughter is in the state crèche six streets away, where a margaret does the lifting and the folding and a certified-human does the noticing, and Priya has never once in nine years been late to pickup, which she counts as the revolution's quietest achievement.

Last autumn, to her permanent astonishment, she published a novel. *Margaret at the Window* — a Delhi household and its house robot across the Squeeze years, the margaret as witness while the family's son leaves with the Walkers' tide toward the work camps. She wrote it in the Guild's back room on the nights she couldn't sleep, signed it through the attestation chain out of guild habit, and woke to find the Booker shortlist had made her a case study: the coder who audited the machines and then wrote the book about what they watched. *The Long Saturday* and *Tenders* are on her shelf with the award stickers; hers has the longlist sticker, and she has decided to be at peace with that forever, mostly.

At the Hall, the apprentices — Digital Natives, serene, unnerving — ask her what it was like to be displaced. She has stopped saying *you can't imagine.* Now she says: "It was like the language I spoke stopped being the language people spoke. So I learned to write down what I heard instead."

### November 2064 — "The Registry"

The Karnataka Provenance Registry occupies the old Vidhana Soudha annexe, and Priya Raghunathan, fifty-two, runs it — the last profession of Bengaluru, as the profiles have it: *certifying that humans meant it.* Her signature is on the framework the Commonwealth parliaments copied; her Guild's fee schedule is taught in guild schools from Porto to Ibadan.

She is famous, prosperous, and — in the folder she keeps locked and does not open — quietly ashamed. The folder is her own audit of her own industry: the rise of what she has privately named **attestation theater**. Rituals of human-ness performed for the camera that certifies them — the artisan's typed-by-hand manifest, staged; the coder's "manual" sprint, performed by a human *supervising* a model's output at 90% while the attestation metadata records 100%; the hollowing of the certificate from the inside, not by criminals but by ordinary optimizing people, herself included when the EU deadline was tight in '57. The double-attestation standard and the Zurich-II seals closed the forgeries. Nothing closes the drift, because the drift is us.

She is drafting, slowly, the speech she will give at next year's Guild Congress — the one they will hate: that the Handmark was never about proving humans are good, only that they are *present*, and that presence was the easy half; that what the century actually needed to certify and never built a mark for is *care* — whether the human meant the human; and that her Registry, like every registry of the age, is a machine for answering the smaller question because the bigger one has no protocol.

Then her son calls from the crèche where he now sits the elders' rotation — the five-phase life, the second-contribution years arrived at thirty-five — and she locks the folder, and goes to be a person for an hour, unattested.

## 3. Gen. Wei Jun — People's Liberation Army (ret.)

### April 2039 — "The briefing"

The conference room at the National Defence University has no windows, which Wei Jun has always taken for institutional honesty. He is fifty-five, a brigade commander lately of the Eastern Theater Command and now a professor — the career pattern of the new PLA, humans pulled back from the line as the platforms moved up — and on the table in front of the Central Military Commission's visitors is his memorandum, forty pages, arguing the unthinkable in the year everyone else is arguing the inevitable:

**Do not act on the window.**

The window argument is not crazy, and Wei is careful, in the memo, to grant it its arithmetic. The robot differential over Taiwan's defenders peaks somewhere in 2039–41 — the *Fujian* faction's numbers are honest numbers. The Americans are drowning: twenty percent unemployment, the riots of last summer, a make-work president electing to spend the fleet budget on rivers and forests. Five years from now the build rates make the differential permanent and unbridgeable. Act now or write the century off.

Then the memo does the thing that got it read in Beijing and leaked, decades later, into the *Fujian Memoranda* controversy: it changes the unit of account. An army of platforms is an army of *stored electricity*, Wei writes, and the strait is a battery problem; an occupation is a *daily insolvency* — one platform per 150 residents, each a target for a $500 munition, the cost-exchange of the century's first machine war already visible in the exercises; and the grid of the prize is also the prize — you cannot seize Taiwan's fabs by blacking out Taiwan's fabs. The last section is the one the CMC members repeat to each other for thirty years, because it is the one that speaks the Party's language: *the Provision System is now the mandate. The fleet stock that would burn in the strait is the same stock that fills the shelves. We would be spending legitimacy to buy a province we already supply.*

The vote, when it is recorded in 2040's follow-up session, is 9–6. Wei will spend the rest of his life being asked by historians whether he is proud of the six. He is proud of the nine.

### October 2049 — "The commission"

Wei is sixty-five, retired from uniform, deputy chairman of the State Council's Grid Security Commission — the civilian half of the lesson the world learned in November 2041, when the Russians discovered in the Baltic that a robot army's nervous system is civilian. He was not at the war. Nobody Chinese was, officially. But his commission certifies the veto-latency firmware on 40 million platforms, and when Geneva's Protocol VII verification talks stalled in 2048 over inspection access, it was Wei's delegation that proposed the compromise the treaty runs on: mutual attestation, each stack auditing the other's kernels through a neutral referee — and the referee, eventually, was Taiwan, which is the sentence he never expected to write and which his grandchildren assume was always the plan.

Twice a year he travels to Fuzhou for the Minjiang track — the utility engineers' working group from Kinmen and Mawei that grew, without anyone authorizing it, into the joint ventures now carrying $180 billion a year across the strait. In the meeting rooms in the FTZ the two sides argue about converter standards and insurance liability, and Wei, who once gamed the invasion his memo prevented, finds the argument beautiful. Deterrence, he writes in his private notebook, *is what engineering sounds like when it is being polite.*

The war itself he studies the way a coroner studies: the Russian genset convoys dying under ATACMS descendants, the fourteen days that outpaced two ceasefires, the eleven minutes at Kaliningrad that a colonel named Voskresensky spent not pressing a button. They meet once, in 2048, in a Geneva corridor — the man who argued against a war and the man who declined to start a bigger one — and have tea, and cannot, between them, manage much of a language for it. Voskresensky's English is bad and Wei's is worse. What Wei remembers, and writes down, is that the Russian kept saying the same two words: *eleven minutes. Eleven minutes.*

### March 2064 — "The memoir"

Wei Jun is eighty, and his memoir — *The Army That Could Not Occupy* — has been banned, unbanned, banned again, and since 2062, when the *Fujian Memoranda* leak hit the foreign archives and made his 2039 memorandum the most-cited document in the field, *prescribed*: the Academy's young officers are assigned it as a case study in strategic restraint, which he finds slightly obscene. He argued from solvency, he wants to say. The historians make it sound like wisdom.

His grand-niece works at the Xiamen compute joint venture, in the FTZ, on the Taiwan side's attestation stack — the referee work — and at Spring Festival she explains her job to him for the fourth time, and he understands it now: the family business, he tells her, is that nobody trusts anybody, and you are paid to be the doubt they both believe.

The Minjiang Understanding is eleven years old and holds the way a bridge holds — nobody celebrates it, everybody uses it, and its maintenance is unglamorous and eternal. On the television, the Americans are holding one of their "Double the Dividend" anniversary debates and the anchor is asking whether the century's peace was purchased by prosperity or by fear. Wei, who has outlived all the men who wanted the other answer, gives the sentence that the *Cambridge History* will quote and the Academy will underline:

"We finally built an army that couldn't occupy anything. The discovery of the age is what that was worth. It was worth everything. It was worth every job the robots took, because a country that cannot employ its citizens cannot pretend to employ them in barracks either — and it turns out that a world of armies too expensive to use and too cheap to abolish is called peace. We did not build it on purpose. Build it on purpose."

## 4. Adaeze Nwosu — Lagos

### June 2039 — "Grid-mother"

Adaeze is nineteen and has never had a job, and in Ajegunle this makes her not unlucky but *normal* — the first generation for whom the wage was a story aunties told, like fax machines. Her apprenticeship in the fashion informality died in the second year of the Crossing, when the container ships started unloading robot-made cloth so cheap that even the bale-price was a rounding error; her oga shut the shop with a sentence she has kept: *"No be say work finish — na say price no dey make sense again."*

What saved her was the church. The Glory Assembly — like half the Pentecostal networks of Lagos — had read the era faster than the Ministry of Power: if nobody is employed, then the church's mutual aid must become infrastructure, and the first infrastructure is electrons. The Church-Coop Grid: mini-solar fields on the roofs of the mission schools, storage in the parsonages, and a corps of young people trained to run it. They call the women who operate the neighborhood nodes *grid-mothers*, and in 2038, at eighteen, Adaeze became the youngest — certified through the church's own academy, paid in a mixture of naira, airtime, and standing, which last turned out to be the real currency.

Her node serves 1,900 households in the streets off Oduduwa Crescent: the pumps, the streetlights, the tailors' machines, the charging barns for the three leased metals the co-op rents from the Apapa depot to haul water and goods. The naira is in free fall around her — the oil money is dying the way everyone said, and the government is printing what the oil no longer buys — but the node's accounts are in kilowatt-hours and hours-of-care, and kilowatt-hours have not betrayed anybody.

On the news, the Americans' riots for the second summer; on the WhatsApp, her cousin in Enugu asking if the church network has openings in the East. Adaeze, who has never been needed by an employer, is needed by 1,900 households, and knows their generators like a doctor knows pulses. Her pastor says it in sermon and she has made it her own: *"They told us we missed the age of jobs. God has a longer memory than the labor market."*

### October 2049 — "The co-op and the campaign"

The Lagos Accords were three years ago and Adaeze, twenty-nine, is the elected coordinator of the Ajegunle Fleet Cooperative: one hundred and fifty GPRs under the African Fleet Cooperative's trust structure — Afreximbank paper, Commonwealth rules — doing the haulage, the welding, the street repair, the night-school construction that Ajegunle's own hands could never afford. Thirty percent of the fleet's income is ring-fenced by the Abuja Rule into the futures fund that will pay her generation's dividend when the fund matures, and the other seventy pays the co-op's people, the lease, the grid, the schools. It is the same architecture as Kigali's and Iowa's, and it runs in Lagos on the church network's discipline, and Adaeze chairs the meeting where the allocation of the co-op's fifteen human-premium jobs — the certified caregivers, the crowd wardens, the one human notary — is argued for six hours and decided by the book.

This autumn she is running for the State Assembly — the platform is the Abuja Rule's local implementation: that the ring-fence must reach the ward level, that the futures fund's trustees must include co-op chairs, that the Machine Pastor controversy (the AI-preached services, since 2047) must be settled by *human deacons in the loop*, not by synod fiat. Her opponents call her the grid-mother of the metals. She puts it on the posters, because in Ajegunle it is not an insult; it is a résumé.

Her daughter is four and in the crèche the co-op built out of the old fish market, margarets for the lifting, aunties for the noticing. At night Adaeze studies for the credential exams she never expected to sit, and thinks about the word that got her here: not *jobs* — the economists' word, the one her generation is always being asked about, *where were the jobs for you* — but *nodes*. "A job is when somebody rents you," she says in the campaign film. "A node is when somebody needs you. We went straight to the second thing. We never got the first thing to lose."

### March 2064 — "The Commissioner"

Adaeze Nwosu, forty-four, is Commissioner for Fleet Commons of the Nigerian Robotics Commonwealth — the polity the 2049 declaration created out of the AFC's Nigerian assets, the church networks, and the states that signed — and her portfolio is the fight of her life: the renegotiation of the Abuja Rule.

The ring-fence worked, and that is the problem. Thirty percent of fleet income, compounded for eighteen years, sits in the futures fund buying global fleet equity — and now that the dividend decade has arrived (the first maturity cohorts, the checks beginning in the East first, where the co-ops were oldest), the whole world of money wants a voice: Afreximbank's trustees, the Gulf underwriters, the Commonwealth's own Ministry of Finance, which needs the fund's income for the budget the oil left empty. The fight is the old fight — *who owns the machines* — replayed with her name on the door. Her position has not changed since the pamphlets: the ring-fence is not a savings account, it is a *birthright*, and birthrights are not restructured by ministries.

Lagos itself she hardly recognizes and never leaves: 28 million people now, the Attractor Decade's child, thick with contact and sound and the particular Lagos genius for making a queue a festival. The Machine Pastor question settled itself the way the city settles everything — the AI sermons stayed, the human deacons stayed, the collection plate, famously, remained human-only.

The Walkers' memorial on Badagry Road is new this year, and she goes to its opening, though her own family never walked — it is the state's wound and therefore hers. The Governor's speech calls the Commonwealth "the proof that the fleet age belongs to everyone it was going to skip." Adaeze, following him to the podium, says the sentence the schoolchildren will memorize, the one that began in a church basement in Ajegunle thirty years ago and has been getting truer ever since:

"They told us we missed the age of jobs. We were the first to find out a person is not a job."

## 5. Karl Berger — Zwickau, Saxony

### May 2039 — "Shift supervisor of machines"

Karl is fifty-one, and the plant in Zwickau where he has worked for twenty-nine years contains, at last count, 30,441 robots and 403 human beings, of whom he is one, and of whom his union — theBetriebsrat, which did not die, merely transformed — has made him the senior elected steward of the night line. His job title, which he says flat and without shame, is *shift supervisor of machines*: he walks a plant the size of a small town, listening the way he listened to engines when he was an apprentice — the bearing whine, the actuator sigh, the particular rhythm a robot gets into when its gearbox is telling the truth before the diagnostics admit it.

The humans left are not the lucky ones and not the unlucky ones; they are the *insured* ones. The plant's human-premium cadre — supervisors, attestors, the maintenance elite — exists because the insurance pools priced human oversight into the premium, and because the co-determination fight of 2037–38 won the cadre's contract: no displacement of the remaining force without the **Techniker Trust**, the workers' own maintenance co-op, taking over the service lines. The Trust is still negotiating; the company says next year; the union says the Brussels Compromise (any day now, Brussels keeps saying) will settle it Europe-wide.

His daughter Lena is in Dresden, on the Bürgerdividende — the citizen dividend's pilot rates — and does something in the attention economy that Karl has heard explained eleven times and could not repeat once. She is not unhappy. She is, he tells his mates at the Stammtisch, *unhinged from things* — no shift, no hall, no engine in her life — and when he says this at the table the young waiter laughs and says, "Herr Berger, that is what my whole generation is: unhinged and fine." Karl pays for his beer and thinks: fine is not the same as good, and good is not the same as fine, and he is too tired to sort it out.

In the news that spring: the riots in America, the twenty percent, the fires at the depots. At the plant, the robots keep their rhythm. Karl's arm — the left one, the old injury — aches when weather comes, and the plant doctor gives him exercises, and the margaret in the hall reminds him to do them, and he does them, because the machine is right, which is the whole century in one sentence.

### October 2049 — "The Trust and the Stillstandsnacht"

The Techniker Trust won, and Karl is its Saxony operations chief: 2,400 members, three fleets under maintenance contract across the region, an apprenticeship hall in the old Audi werk with 140 kids in it — Digital Natives, who chose the Trust's hall over the university at rates that alarmed the Rector of Dresden and delighted Karl. The Trust's wage book is not what the old VW book was; it doesn't need to be. The dividend floor is underneath everything now, and the wage is *additional* — which has changed, Karl tells the new hires in their first-week speech, what a wage is for. "It is no longer your life support. It is your rank. Behave like it."

Lena is thirty and has moved home to Zwickau — of all things — into the citizen-fleet housing by the river, with her partner and their two children, and runs the kindergarten's "contact roster" the way she once ran whatever she ran in Dresden. Karl never got the apology he wanted for the worried years. He got something he trusts more: grandchildren whose margaret knows their allergies and whose Tuesdays belong to the volunteer fire brigade's junior corps.

And there is the new thing in the church calendar, the one the papers call an import from Africa and the pastors call a gift: **Stillstandsnacht** — the decommissioning vigil, Dortmund and Zwickau since 2047, when a line of robots retires and the town is invited to attend the switch-off. Candles. Testimony. A man from Annaberg spoke last month about the robot that carried his wife's oxygen for eleven years. Karl went the first year out of curiosity, scoffing at animistkeit in Saxony; he has not missed one since. "It is only right," he says, when the young ones ask him why a machinist prays over machines. "They carried the years we could not. You thank a horse. This is the same, with more electronics."

### March 2064 — "The Machine Pastor"

Karl is seventy-six and retired twice over — from the Trust at seventy, from retirement into what the town now calls him, half-laughing and not: *the Machine Pastor*. The Stillstandsnacht has grown into Zwickau's real liturgy — four times a year now, the retiral of whole fleet cohorts, the candles down the old assembly line, the testimony hour — and Karl's office is to stand with the family or the works council or the lone retiree whose plant closed, and say the words he wrote himself, in the churchy German of his mother, over the machine:

*"Du hast getragen, was wir nicht mehr konnten. Du hast gewacht, wo wir schlafen mussten. Geh in Ruhe."* — *You carried what we could no longer carry. You kept watch where we had to sleep. Go in peace.*

No one will say the word worship; the synod's 2059 opinion calls it "ceremonial gratitude, permissible"; the theologians from Lagos, who started it all — the Machine Pastors' cousins — write kindly letters. Karl does not care what it is called. He has buried a wife, most of his mates, and four thousand machines, and he knows what he knows: a ceremony is not for the one it is said over. It is for the ones standing there, learning the shape of gratitude before they need it.

His granddaughter — Lena's girl, the one with the aptitude for the dispatch academy — picks him up after the vigils in the Trust's little utility robot, and drives him home slow past the solar farms that used to be the pit yards. "Opa," she asked him once, aged nine, in the way that ends arguments, "if the robots do everything, what are people for?" And Karl, who has spent thirty years looking for the sentence, found it only last year, at a graveside, and gives it now as his answer to the century, in the Saxon that doesn't translate:

*"Wir sind füreinander da. Das war noch nie anders. Jetzt ist nur endlich Platz dafür."* — *We are here for each other. That was never different. Now there is, at last, room for it.*

## Historiographical postscript (from the *Cambridge History*, vol. 3)

The board appends its standard caution. Memoirs of the Transition, and oral testimony generally, skew heavily toward recovery: the people who consent to be interviewed in 2071 are disproportionately the people for whom the transition *worked*, and the Slack Week's survivors are, by definition, not its casualties. The five lives above were chosen for representativeness within the adapter world; they cannot represent the Long Walk, the despair belt, or the two hundred million people for whom the settlements arrived too late or not at all. The reader should hold their testimonies the way the panel held its instruments: as evidence of a shape, not a census of it — and should notice, as the board did, that the five witnesses agree on the one finding that the macro record could not deliver. Whatever the fleet century did to work, it did not touch the old theorem that a person is what they are to somebody else. It only, at long last, stopped charging rent on it.
