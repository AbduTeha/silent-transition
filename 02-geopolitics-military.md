## 1. The new power equation

The Crossing did not add a new instrument of power; it **re-based the old four**. Between 2034 and 2044, every serious foreign ministry rebuilt its national assessments around a single identity:

> **Power = Energy × Compute × Fleets × Governance-Speed**

Not a sum — a product. The multiplication matters: a zero in any factor zeroes the whole expression. Russia entered the Transition with a superb energy score and a compute score near zero; the product was a middle power with grievances, which is precisely how the Two-Week War happened. Multiplication also explains the decade's great surprise: the polities that rose fastest were not the richest but the ones with **no zero and a high fourth factor**.

The four currencies, defined operationally:

- **Energy** — firm, cheap, sovereign electricity per capita, deliverable at data-center scale. The canon numbers frame it: world electricity 32,000 TWh (Y0) → 48,000 TWh (Y10) → 78,000 TWh (Y30), with AI data centers alone at ~1,000 TWh (Y0) → 6,500 TWh (Y10). A country that cannot power a frontier cluster at $0.02/kWh does not have an energy policy; it has an energy handicap.
- **Compute** — frontier training capacity *plus* fleet-grade inference at home. Training gets the headlines; inference wins wars, because by Y10 the 480M installed GPRs run on domestic silicon or they do not run at all.
- **Fleets** — owned or credibly co-determined robot capital stock, military and industrial. One GPR ≈ 2.4 effective human-FTE (errata, 08); fleets are therefore denominated in *people-equivalents*, which is why they are strategic, not industrial, assets.
- **Governance-speed** — the underrated fourth factor: the months required to restructure law and the fiscal base when the payroll tax base evaporates. Measured bluntly: *time from decision to a working fleet-levy or dividend instrument.* Norway: 14 months. UAE: 5. Estonia: 9. China: 7 — decree-fast, but with unpriced implementation debt that surfaced as Provision System strains (errata: the transition is rockier than it looks). The United States: 38 months, eaten by litigation — yet the same courts produced the Fleet Protocol (2048), the era's most consequential legal export. The EU: 51 months under unanimity, until the Brussels Compromise (2040) built a fast lane around itself. Governance-speed is the factor every 2026-era forecast omitted, because it is invisible in GDP and decisive in Y5–Y10.

**Who converts wealth into capability fastest?** Three answers, in ascending order of surprise:

1. **The Gulf converts fastest per dollar.** Capital × sun × five decision-makers: Abu Dhabi and Riyadh went from compute score 3 to 8 in ten years (Abu Dhabi Compute Zone; Saudi Project Verdant), a conversion rate no democracy matched. Their constraint was never money or photons; it was personnel, and fleets eventually solved that too.
2. **China converts fastest at scale.** The CGRG consolidation (2037) verticalized the world's largest robot production base — China held roughly 15M/yr of the world's 40M/yr capacity at Y5 — and the state's balance sheet absorbed the transition's social costs faster than any market could. The errata is binding here: speed bought brittleness. The Wuxi riots and the Y6–Y9 Provision System strains are the invoice for that 7-year fiscal restructure.
3. **The EU is the panel's contrarian pick for most improved.** From governance-speed 2 (Y0) to 6 (Y10), via the Brussels Compromise's fleet co-determination, Meridian Industrial's consortium model, and the Mediterranean Grid Compact (2038). Europe converted *law* into deployment speed — the only power that did it that direction.

The United States remains the largest absolute power in every column but is the *least efficient converter* among the leaders: federalism plus courts add years, and years in Y3–Y8 were the currency of lives. Russia is the cautionary row: the product of its scores collapsed from mid-tier to marginal in a decade, which is the arithmetic precondition of Section 4.

## 2. The Silicon Curtain

**The Singapore Compute Compact (Nov 2035)** was the Crossing's first great institutional act. Signed in eleven months from first cable — unprecedented speed, and itself evidence of the threat perception — it bound the US, Japan, Korea, the Netherlands, the UK, Taiwan, Singapore (as licensing seat), Canada and Australia to a common control list: training runs above **2×10²⁸ FLOP**, advanced packaging and HBM stacks, >400G optical interconnect, and the litho-adjacent toolchain. The chokepoints were real in Y1: the Compact members then controlled an estimated 87% of frontier-relevant compute supply. Singapore's port, already the physical chokepoint of Asian trade, became its informational twin — the **Singapore List**, administered by a new Compute Licensing Authority with more day-to-day power over the world economy than OPEC at its peak.

**China's retaliation was not to race the list but to erase its market.** The **Qwen Shock** (2036): Beijing released frontier-class open weights — free, permissively licensed, runnable on sovereign hardware anywhere — in a rolling series that within 18 months had 60+ countries operating capable domestic instances. The strategic logic was elegant and, at first, widely misread in Washington as desperation: where the Compact controls *hardware*, open weights control *expectations*. Why license a US API at $11/M tokens when an equal-for-90%-of-use-cases model runs locally at electricity cost? Frontier leadership survived — the Compact's labs kept a genuine 2–3 generation lead at the absolute top through Y8 — but leadership over a free good is a strange kind of wealth. US and allied API revenues fell 70% in real terms within three years.

**Why export control failed by Y12.** The panel's consensus explanation is the **electricity analogy**. Electrification diffused slowly (four decades) because each factory had to be physically rebuilt around the motor. Compute diffused in five years because the "factory" — the model — is pure information; weights move at the speed of a courier or a memory stick, and the Qwen Shock had already put the important weights outside the wall. Four mechanisms did the rest:

- **The demand side outgrew the wall.** By Y12 there were 700M installed GPRs (canon). Every one needs inference. The Compact's own firms could not forgo 70% of the world market, and enforcement eroded into a licensing-revenue regime.
- **The workaround matured.** Chinese DUV multi-patterning plus chiplet architectures reached roughly 60% of frontier efficiency at ~3× the energy cost by Y10 — and energy was the one input China had in surplus (9,000+ TWh system by Y15).
- **Members defected at the margin.** Dutch litho, Korean HBM, Singaporean licensing — every Compact capital had a national industry whose best customers were outside the wall. By Y9 the List was enforced mainly against small neutral states, which is how regimes die.
- **Open weights are a leaky dam.** A model released once is a model released forever. Each successive Qwen-generation release re-flooded the middle of the market; the Compact was defending the top 5% of a distribution that had already left.

By Y12 (2046) the assessment in all five panel sections is identical: **the Curtain was a revenue fence, not a capability wall.** Capability converged; the diffusion lag settled at ~18 months between frontier and free.

**What the Curtain left behind — the verification divide.** The Compact's enduring legacy was not prohibition but *attestation*. The 2039 "Singapore Pivot" converted the licensing machinery from export control to **provenance infrastructure**: model evaluation laboratories, weights-provenance chains, attested firmware for every GPR sold into member markets, insurance-grade audit. When the Fleet Protocol Foundation took over Pantheon OS (2048), the attestation stack came with it. China built the parallel **Zhuangzhou attestation stack** under CGRG. The Y30 world thus runs on two convergent capabilities and two *incompatible trust architectures* — two plug standards for reality. This is the divide that matters: it shapes which robots can be insured, which weapons count as treaty-compliant (Section 7), and which countries can referee between the stacks. That last role is Taiwan's (Section 3), and it is worth more than the silicon shield ever was.

## 3. Taiwan: the window that wasn't taken

**The deterrence problem, restated by machines.** Pre-Crossing, the strait (130 km at its narrowest) made amphibious assault a maritime suicide pact; the PLA's plausible options were quarantine and grey-zone pressure. The Crossing seemed to change this. By Y5, China's robot production capacity (~15M/yr) dwarfed Taiwan's (≈60k/yr), and the US fleet build was being consumed by the Civil Works Emergency, Grid Riots summers, and 20.5% peak unemployment. The PLA's **"robot differential window" (2039–41)** — argued most forcefully in the 2040 *Fujian Memoranda* (see Section 9b) — held that only in this interval could China land a machine army without first fighting one: after Y8, US re-industrialization at 120M/yr production capacity would close the differential forever.

**Why the window argument lost.** Five reasons, in the order the Politburo's April 2041 session weighed them:

1. **A robot army is an electricity army, and the strait is a battery problem.** Landing 200,000 platforms requires ~1.8M tonnes of roll-on sealift (the PLA Navy had ~0.5M), 2+ GW of landing-zone charging infrastructure, and an energy supply line that every satellite would watch. Amphibious logistics did not get easier because the soldiers were gone; it got *more* brittle, because a platform at 20% charge is a worse loss than a fatigued soldier.
2. **Grid fragility cuts both ways.** An occupation needs the occupied grid — no army generators a 23M-person economy. But Taiwan's islanded grid (≈45 GW peak) was also the substrate of its fabs. Destroying the grid to seize the fabs destroys the prize; running the grid means being the electricity supplier of a population you have just dispossessed.
3. **Robot armies cannot hold cities cheaply.** Crowd control at one platform per ~150 residents implies ~155,000 platforms for Taiwan's population — each a $1.8M target against $500 loitering munitions (the cost-exchange that Yemen would prove a year later at 1:6,000, Section 7). An occupier loses money every day of occupation, and the insurgency is robot-armed: the unemployed of the Transition have unlimited time and cheap drones.
4. **The silicon shield was already dissolving.** Taiwan's leading-edge fab share fell from ~90% (Y0) to 82% (Y4) to 55% (Y10) as capacity diversified — Arizona (three fabs), Kumamoto (two), Dresden (ESMC), Gujarat (Dholera) — driven by the customers' own supply-security panic after the Compact. Invading in 2041 to seize fabs that were leaving would only accelerate the exodus and, under sanctions, strand the remainder. The shield's erosion (Y4–Y10) made the *incentive* for war peak in exactly the years when the *feasibility* calculus was collapsing.
5. **China's own fragility (errata 1).** The Provision System was straining (Y6–9), the Wuxi riots were fresh, and the Digital Mandate — legitimacy from provision performance — could not survive a failed adventure. The leadership chose the only bet that did not require winning: infrastructure.

**The dangerous decade (2038–48)** ran on a quarterly crisis rhythm: quarantine exercises (2039), the post-Meridian-7 opportunism scare (spring 2041, when the $2.1T crash hit Taipei hardest), the succession-year tensions of 2047. It passed for the reasons above — plus one quiet construction project. From 2043, Kinmen and Mawei utility engineers began coordinating cross-strait grid-reliability protocols; this "Minjiang track" (after the river through Fuzhou) grew into commercial joint compute ventures in the **Fujian Free-Trade Zone** — Fuzhou data parks, joint Huaqing–Atlas assembly at Mawei — that by Y20 carried $180B/yr in flows.

**The Minjiang Understanding (2055)** is the endpoint the panel actually observed: not a treaty, not recognition, not unification — **ambient peace**. Sovereignty frozen without a name; daily flights; the FTZ ventures; and Taiwan re-founded as the world's **attestation referee** — its evaluation laboratories, trusted by both the Pact and Zhuangzhou stacks, certify models and firmware for both blocs. The island lost the silicon shield and kept the more valuable one: *verification neutrality*. The Understanding holds because by 2055 neither side can win cheaply, both are rich through Fujian, and every fleet on earth needs someone both stacks trust.

## 4. The Two-Week War (Nov 2041)

**Why declining Russia gambled.** The arithmetic was merciless and the Kremlin read it correctly. Oil demand peaked in 2039 (canon); the Urals basket fell 45% in real terms from Y0 to Y7 and the decline steepened every quarter as fleets ate the last transport demand. Arms exports — the second pillar — were collapsing: robot-era buyers wanted platforms and attested kernels, not 2030s armor. Domestic GPR output was trivial (~300k/yr, mostly licensed Chinese chassis). The tech gap widened annually, and the Russian staff's own studies (the *Sever Plan*) concluded the window for *any* conventional gain closed by Y9, when NATO's platform ramp would make the correlation hopeless. Meanwhile the West looked distracted: the Meridian-7 crash (Mar 2041, $2.1T), Boone's "American Rebuild" make-work politics, European societies at 15%+ displacement unemployment. The gamble: **one clean fait accompli** — seize the 65-km Suwałki corridor, reconnect Kaliningrad by land, and present NATO with a ground reality its unemployed publics would not pay blood to reverse. The plan's core assumption was *human* politics: that NATO would answer with sanctions, not war.

**Order of battle — the first machine-major war.** Both sides fought the corridor battle predominantly with machines; humans were kept **30+ km behind** the line of contact (Russian command in Kaliningrad and Belarus, NATO's in Polish and Lithuanian rear areas).

| Element | Russia | NATO | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Humanoid combat platforms | ~1,500 (Vyuga line; Chinese-origin chassis) | ~900 by Day 7 (Polish *Husarz*, Franco-German *Lynx-X*, US *Ares* defense derivatives of Kinetiq chassis) | ~2,400 committed in theater |
| Drones (ISR / strike / loitering) | ~26,000 | ~14,000 | 40,000 total; loitering munitions $400–900 |
| Human soldiers in contact | <400 (specialists) | <300 | Manpower prestige's funeral |
| True constraint | Diesel gensets for field charging | Microreactors + grid taps | Diesel convoys became the targeting signature |
| 14-day budget | ~$190B | ~$240B | A cheap world-historical war |

**The 14-day tempo (Nov 3–17, 2041).** The defining feature was speed relative to *human* decision cycles. Platform engagement cycles ran ~4 seconds; the slowest node in either war machine was the 90-minute emergency committee. By the time the US National Security Council finished its first session on Day 2, the Russian spearhead had moved 40 km. Day 1: breach along the corridor; Day 2: Article 4 consultations become Article 5; Day 3: Marijampolė reached, 68 km in 60 hours; Days 4–6: NATO abandons force-on-force platform exchange and goes after Russian *electrons* — the genset convoys, the Kaliningrad interconnectors; Days 7–9: Russian platforms starve (−40% battery efficiency in freezing fog), NATO's own platform arrivals tip the exchange; Days 10–14: encirclement at Marijampolė, ceasefire on the Vilnius line, November 17. Fourteen days start to finish — the war outpaced its own diplomacy: two ceasefire proposals lapsed because the front they described no longer existed.

**The Kaliningrad Clock (Day 5, Nov 8).** The war's hinge was almost not on the battlefield. An automated long-range air-defense battery outside Kaliningrad — autonomous engagement authorized by a 2040 doctrine update that devolved fire decisions below command level — detected and engaged a NATO EW aircraft (an RC-135 derivative, callsign HOMER 31) over international Baltic waters. The battery's launch queue included a human veto window of **11 minutes** for over-water engagements — a paper requirement nobody expected to matter. The duty officer, Col. A. Voskresensky, was mid-shift-change, found the queue, and vetoed at T-minus 11. Had the missile flown, NATO's automated response against Kaliningrad's air-defense grid — and the escalation ladder beyond it, both sides' platforms wired to their national early-warning nervous systems — is the counterfactual every subsequent treaty is built to prevent. The arms-control community calls it simply **the Clock**; Section 7's veto-latency standard is named for its duration.

**Casualties: ~11,000 dead** — Russian support and logistics crews ~6,300; Lithuanian and Polish civilians ~2,600; NATO personnel ~1,100; other ~1,000. The first war in modern history where the losing side's humans died overwhelmingly at the logistics tail, not the front.

**Consequences.**

- **Geneva Protocol VII (2042):** human-in-the-loop requirements for lethal autonomy, negotiated in eight months — the fastest law ever written (fear is a great drafter). Its central provision, the **Voskresensky Rule**, mandates a minimum human veto latency for strategic-class engagements.
- **The war's political verdict fell on Boone** — "defeated after the war" (canon) — establishing the era's pattern: publics forgive machine wars their treasure but not their dead, and vote on the dividend, not the flag.
- **Russia's "battery state" decline.** Sanctions plus robot-irrelevance closed every path except the endowment: hydrocarbons, minerals, grain — managed decline as a resource warehouse. By Y15, Moscow's own strategists coined the term: a state that is, functionally, a battery — stored value, discharging slowly.
- **The Yenisei Arrangement (2049):** Siberian resources-for-fleets with Beijing. Russia becomes, quietly and formally, China's resource appendix (canon) — the power equation's product, computed to its floor.

## 5. Alliance architecture after labor

The Transition dissolved the old alliance question (who fights whom) and replaced it with a new one (whose machines can talk to whom). Four structures emerged; all are infrastructure blocs wearing security costumes.

- **The Compute Pact** — the Singapore Compact's positive-agenda successor (charter: Seoul, Jun 2037): joint compute buildout, mutual attestation recognition, coordinated fleet standards. Core members: **US, Japan, Korea, Netherlands, UK, Taiwan (remnant institutions — the attestation labs and offshore-fab holdings), Canada, Australia**; associates: Singapore, Switzerland, Israel, Norway.
- **The Grid Union** — the institutional body of the **Mediterranean Grid Compact (2038)**: EU capital + Gulf capital + Maghreb sun, HVDC under the Mediterranean (60 GW by Y10, 140 GW by Y20; Sahara arrays at $0.007/kWh). Europe imports 1,900 TWh/yr through it by Y20. It is the era's most morally entangled institution: **Europe buys electrons instead of migrants** — paying Maghreb and Gulf states a stability rent in current rather than absorbing their displaced labor — and calls the arrangement a climate partnership, which it also is.
- **The Shanghai Compute Organization (SCO-C, charter Mar 2039):** China, Russia, Iran, Pakistan, plus Belarus, Kazakhstan and Central Asian associates — the Zhuangzhou attestation realm with a political roof.
- **The Non-Aligned Compute Movement (NACM, Bali communiqué, 2041):** India, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa — and, partially, the Gulf, which runs both stacks and calls this sovereignty. NACM's real product is the **Non-Aligned Verification Registry (New Delhi, 2045)**, which certifies cross-stack compliance and quietly became the world's trade court for firmware.

The Hong Kong Fleet Convention (2048) — levy harmonization across all four structures — is the closest thing the era produced to a general constitution for machine labor: a rare moment when the blocs agreed that fleets themselves, not states, are the thing that must be governed.

## 6. Energy geography after oil

**Peak and slide.** Oil demand peaked in 2039 at 104.5 Mb/d — two years after the Labor-Debt Crunch, one year before the Grid Riots, exactly when the historians later said it should have been obvious. The slide was slower than the peak-pessimists predicted (fleets need feedstocks and the Global South motorized late) but relentless: 71 Mb/d by Y15, 38 Mb/d by Y30. Brent-equivalent real price: $78 (Y0) → $52 (Y5) → $31 (Y10) → $19 (Y30). The geographic consequence is the century's largest relative transfer of** strategic rent away from the 25–55° latitudes toward the 15–35° sunbelt.

**The great petro-state divergence — one variable, two fates.** Whether a producer survived the peak depended less on geology than on a single Y0-era fact: **did it have a sovereign fund?**

- **Fund states adapted.** Norway ($2.1T fund at Y0, converted early into compute and fleet equity, $5.6T by Y20) is the clean case. The UAE is the fast case: Abu Dhabi Compute Zone (2041) made the emirate a sovereign compute state — 22 GW owned capacity by Y20. Saudi Arabia is the scale case: **Project Verdant** — 55 GW of desert solar, sovereign compute, and a budget in which oil's share fell from 78% (Y0) to 31% (Y20). The Gulf did not diversify *away from* energy; it moved one step up the value chain, from molecules to electrons to compute cycles — the **Sunbelt Sovereigns** pattern.
- **No-fund states did not.** Iraq (92% oil revenue, a 4.6M-name public payroll) hit a 24%-of-GDP fiscal gap by Y8 and accepted the IMF's Hydrocarbon Transition Facility trusteeship in 2044. Algeria's Sonatrach patronage system went through seven finance ministers in four years. Angola converted Chinese debt into mineral liens. The Venezuela-alikes discovered the cruelest irony of the era: robotized fields could keep pumping with 800 workers instead of 80,000 — at a price floor that no longer existed. Several of these states appear in the Section 8 table as Collapse or Authoritarian capture, and the mechanism is the same in every case: the payroll state had no other fiscal organ.
- **The Sunbelt Sovereigns as new middle powers:** Saudi, UAE, Oman, Morocco — plus Australia (solar HVDC toward Singapore) — became the era's swing actors: rich enough to buy fleets, sunny enough to power them, and courted by both attestation blocs. Morocco is the panel's dark-horse: Grid Union anchor, Sahara arrays, and the Compact's preferred African manufacturing shore.

**Russia's fall** completes the pattern from the other side: energy score 7 → 4 (table, §1), the 2041 gamble, the battery state, and the Yenisei Arrangement's quiet vassalage. A country that produced 11% of world oil at Y0 produces, by Y30, 4% — with most of it flowing east under take-or-pay contracts priced in yuan.

**The Electro-Balkans — the era's minor flashpoint.** Where the Grid Union's plumbing meets the SCO-C's ambitions, Greece and Turkey ran a decade-long "HVDC war" (2044–46 at its hottest): Greece as the Union's southeastern terminus (the 3 GW *Poseidon Link*, Egypt→Crete→Peloponnese, 2045), Turkey building the rival "Anatolian Ring" financed from the east. The fighting never went kinetic between states: sabotage of the Sakarya converter station (2045), insurance-withdrawal spirals, cyber intrusions into converter controls, one naval collision — under 200 dead in total. The **Athens Concords (2047)** ended it with the era's signature compromise: Turkey recognized as a compensated *transit state* for electricity it does not own. The panel scores the Electro-Balkans as the paradigm case of **MAD-2 skirmishing** (Section 7): wars against infrastructure, not armies.

## 7. Militaries when labor is free

**The death of manpower prestige.** For two centuries, mass armies were simultaneously the school of the nation and its jobs program. The Crossing killed both functions in one stroke: a state that cannot employ its citizens in warehouses cannot pretend to employ them in barracks, and a robot army makes the conscript worse than useless — a person to feed, train, insure, and expose to veto-latency liability. **Conscription ended worldwide by Y8 (2042)**: Korea's suspension (2042) was the symbol, Israel's referendum (2041) the surprise, the last holdouts' quiet terminations the anticlimax.

**Shrink the human, balloon the machine.** US active component: 1.28M (Y0) → 410k (Y10) → 260k (Y20), while US military GPRs went 90k (Y5) → 2.1M (Y10) → 6.5M (Y20). PLA: 2.0M → 800k (Y10) → 500k (Y20) humans, ~12M platforms. Recruitment became a **volunteer elite of three professions** — operators (fleet commanders at 1:2,000 platform ratios), maintainers (the largest branch), and ethicists (the *red-key* officers mandated by Protocol VII, drawn from philosophy and law faculties at four times private-sector pay). The academies' entering classes by Y10 were majority engineering-ethics double majors; the panel regards this as the quietest revolution in the whole simulation.

**Grid fragility = MAD-2.** The Two-Week War's deepest lesson was that a robot army's nervous system is civilian: grids, converter stations, and the frontier clusters. By Y10, 71% of frontier training capacity sat in twelve metro-regions. Hence the new deterrence logic: **mutual assured disruption** — every major power's compute-and-energy substrate is hostage to every other's, and the hostage is declared. MAD-2 differs from MAD-1 in both directions: attribution of physical strikes is easy (a destroyed converter is not deniable), but sabotage via ghost fleets and firmware is murkier than any missile silo. Stability came from the informal **Grid Accords (Geneva, 2046)** — no attacks on civilian converter stations or civil data centers, verified by the same attestation infrastructure the trade system uses — and they held, barely, through the Electro-Balkans.

**Swarm economics: the Yemen Drone War (2043).** The poor-side proof arrived two years after the rich-side one. The Sana'a Front (fictional composite) fielded ~90,000 drones at an average unit cost of **$520** against a naval coalition intercepting with weapons averaging **$5M** — a cost-exchange ratio near 1:10,000. The coalition kept the sea lanes and spent $60B destroying perhaps $90M of airframe. The strategic finding, now doctrine everywhere: **insurgents adapt faster than states** because open weights (the Qwen Shock's true legacy) put targeting-grade autonomy on commodity autopilots. The re-balancing took until ~Y15: $3,000 intercept drones, 250-kW microwave trucks, and insurance pools that price unattested swarm risk out of the market — the insurer as silent governor, again.

**The Seoul Regime (2047).** Autonomous-weapons limits, negotiated with Yemen and Kaliningrad both on the wall: lethal engagements require a mandatory **human veto latency** — ≥11 minutes (the Voskresensky window) for strategic-class engagements, ≥90 seconds for tactical — and compliance is verified by **firmware attestation**: weapons platforms ship with signed, auditable kernels from one of the two recognized stacks, with flight-recorded veto logs. Sixty-three states signed by Y20, including China (which wanted the Pact's fleets attested more than it wanted its own unconstrained). Enforcement is economic, not martial: unattested platforms are uninsurable and unexportable, which leaves the holdouts — Iran's navy, assorted gray fleets — outside both the law and the market. The panel's verdict: imperfect, attestation-gapped at the margins, and yet the reason no Kaliningrad Clock has recurred.

**Space.** Launch at $190/kg by Y12 made orbit the obvious place for the two most power-hungry, heat-tolerant workloads: training overflow and the "exocloud" of inference for fleets in transit. **Orbital compute constellations** (2050s): first gigawatt-class cluster 2052, ~8 GW by Y20, ~30 GW by Y30 — the beginning of the off-world industry that, per errata 7, partially breaks the land-rent assumption back home. Two accidents shaped the law: the **Vostok-9 debris event (2047)** (an SCO anti-satellite test that seeded a tracking scare) produced the Vienna Debris Protocol (2048) and hard **ASAT norms** — no debris-generating tests, no targeting of civil constellations; and the **Outer Fleets Accord (2058)** limited orbital weapons platforms, reserved LEO compute for attested civilian fleets, and established the Vienna Outer Registry for the robot fleets working off-world. Space, alone among the domains, was governed *before* it was weaponized — the panel attributes this entirely to the fact that every orbital actor had $80B of constellation directly above its own cities.

## 9. Panel dissent box

> **FORMAL DISSENT (a) — Gen. H. Dalgaard, ret. (NATO planner).** *The Two-Week War was avoidable, and we have misread its lessons twice.* The Suwałki gambit was deterrable in advance: NATO's prewar platform stocks outnumbered Russia's roughly 3:1, but they were positioned for territorial defense of the Baltics, not corridor denial. The forward robot line proposed in 2039 — a 40-km sensor-strike belt across the gap — was deferred for budget during the Civil Works Emergency, and Moscow priced that deferral accurately. On lessons: the recorded conclusion, "machine tempo requires machine decision," drove NATO's automated C2 modernization and is the *wrong* lesson. The war was saved, not lost, by eleven minutes of human veto; the real failure was doctrinal (devolving engagement authority to batteries in 2040), which Protocol VII only partially fixed by regulating the weapon rather than the posture. We regulated the rifle and left the doctrine to the general staffs.

> **FORMAL DISSENT (b) — W. Chen (Chinese security studies).** *The Taiwan window was more real than the record admits.* The official history treats the window faction as cranks outmaneuvered by geography, but the Fujian Memoranda (2040, leaked 2062) show the differential math was closer than the reviewers concede: in 2041 China's deployable platform surplus over Taiwan was roughly 40:1, and the US build was visibly consumed by domestic emergency — Grid Riots summers, 20.5% unemployment, a make-work presidency. The strait, the grid, and the insurgency arithmetic were all genuine, but they were not the binding constraint in 2041; the binding constraint was that the Provision System was straining and the Wuxi riots were fresh, and the Digital Mandate could not survive a war it might win. The window closed because Beijing was *weak*, not because invasion was impossible — and the record's ex-post geography is deterrence theory flattering itself. The policy implication is unwelcome but unavoidable: the next window, if there is one, opens when a strong, provision-secure leadership calculates that the Fujian ventures no longer pay. The Minjiang Understanding is a settlement, not a solution; settlements with no name can also end with none.
